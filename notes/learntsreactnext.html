Before you start

Three rules that decide whether this works

The path below is ordinary. Almost everyone who fails at this fails on these three things, not on the content.

1 · Build, don't watch

Reading and watching feel like progress and aren't. Target roughly one third input, two thirds building. If you finish a stage with nothing you can open in a browser, you haven't finished it.

2 · Respect the order

Each stage assumes the one before. Skipping to React with shaky JavaScript is the single most common cause of "React is confusing" — the confusion is usually about references, closures or async, not React.

3 · Read primary sources

The official docs for all three are genuinely excellent now, which was not true a few years ago. A course is a supplement to react.dev and nextjs.org/docs, not a replacement.

◆ On the hour estimates. They assume focused, hands-on time and prior programming experience of some kind. Treat them as relative weights, not a schedule — a complete beginner should expect to roughly double them, and nobody's numbers are anyone else's.

Two ways to run the same path

The ten stages don't change. What changes is depth and how much of the craft layer you take on the first pass. Pick by what you need at the end of it, not by ambition.

Track A · 5-week sprint ≈ 200 h

For

Someone who already programs and needs to be productive in a React/Next codebase quickly — a backend dev moving to full-stack, or anyone with a dated deadline.

Covers

Stages 00–07 at working depth, plus the minimum of 08–09 needed to ship one deployed app.

Consciously skips

Conditional/mapped types, i18n, deep testing, design-system work, Core Web Vitals budgets. You'll know these exist and where to come back to them.

Ends with

One deployed Next.js app you can talk through, and no gaps in the language layer.

Track B · 8-week deep dive ≈ 320 h

For

Someone who wants to own decisions, not just close tickets — career switchers, or a dev who will be the one choosing the stack and reviewing others' React.

Covers

All ten stages at the upper end of each estimate, with three build projects instead of one.

Adds over Track A

Generic components, server/client boundary reasoning, caching, testing behaviour, accessibility, i18n, performance budgets, CI and monitoring.

Ends with

A production-shaped app plus the vocabulary to argue about architecture with evidence.

◆ At a different pace? These are hour budgets, not calendars. At 20 h/week the sprint is 10 weeks and the deep dive 16; the sequence and the checkpoints are identical. A complete beginner should expect to roughly double either number.

Everything here is free and primary. A paid course adds pace pressure you already have.

Guardrails that make 5 weeks possible

  • Timebox, don't complete. When a week's hours are gone, move on with notes on what's shaky. The sequence matters more than any single stage's polish.
  • One project, growing. Don't start five apps. Extend the W2 UI into the W5 capstone so each stage lands on code you already understand.
  • Ban the shortcuts that hide the model: no UI kit until W4, no state library, no AI-generated components you can't rewrite by hand.
  • Type as you go. Writing JS "for speed" and typing later costs more than it saves.
  • Stop at the deferred list. If you're reading about conditional types or i18n in week 3, you're off-track — those are Track B.

Known cost of this track

You will be able to build, and you will not yet be able to review. Expect to revisit stages 02, 08 and 09 in your first real codebase — that's the trade, made on purpose.

Track B · deep divewhere the extra ≈ 120 hours go

What the extra three weeks actually buy

Not more topics for their own sake. Three specific capabilities the sprint cannot produce, plus the projects that force them.

1 · Type-level modelling

Generics, discriminated unions and utility types stop being syntax and become a design tool — you make illegal states unrepresentable instead of writing runtime guards.

Where

Weeks 2–3, ~35 h more than the sprint.

2 · Rendering & caching judgement

The sprint teaches you to make Next.js work. This teaches you to predict it: what runs where, what's cached, what invalidates, and why the staging build behaves differently.

Where

Week 5, ~25 h more, all of it hands-on with the network tab open.

3 · Shippability

Tests that survive refactors, an app usable without a mouse, more than one locale, a measured performance budget, and CI that stops you shipping regressions.

Where

Weeks 7–8, ~55 h that the sprint compresses into one afternoon.

Three projects, in this order

  • W4 — typed component library. Five components, no framework. Forces stages 01–04.
  • W6 — data-driven app. Auth, list/detail, mutations, optimistic UI. Forces stages 05–07.
  • W8 — the capstone, hardened. Take the W6 app to tested, accessible, monitored and deployed. Forces stages 08–09.

How to tell you chose the right track

  • Finishing week 3 of the sprint feeling rushed but not lost means the sprint fits.
  • Feeling lost means you needed Track B's week 1–3 pacing. Switch; the stages are the same.
  • Cruising through Track B's first fortnight means you had more JavaScript than you thought — jump to its week 3 and finish in six.
Stage 00 · Language30–50 h

JavaScript you actually need

Not all of JavaScript. The subset that React and TypeScript are built on top of — and the subset whose absence gets misdiagnosed as "React is hard".

Learn properly

  • Values vs references — why [1,2] === [1,2] is false, and why mutating props breaks React
  • Array methods — map, filter, reduce, find, some. You will write these hourly
  • Destructuring, spread, rest — the syntax of every React codebase
  • Closures — the mechanism behind hooks and stale-value bugs
  • Promises & async/await, and roughly what the event loop does
  • ES modules — import/export, default vs named
  • Immutable update patterns for objects and arrays
  • ?. and ??, template literals, Set/Map

Safe to defer

  • Prototype-chain internals and class inheritance depth
  • this beyond "arrow functions don't rebind it"
  • Generators, proxies, decorators, Symbol
  • jQuery-era manual DOM manipulation
  • Bundler internals — the framework handles this now

Checkpoint

Without help: explain why two identical arrays aren't equal, rewrite a nested for loop as a reduce, and fetch from an API with async/await including the error path. If any of those is a struggle, stay here — this stage pays compound interest.

Stage 01 · Language15–25 h

TypeScript fundamentals

TypeScript is a description of what your JavaScript already does. Most of learning it is learning to stop writing types and let inference work.

Core

  • Primitive annotations, arrays, tuples, objects
  • Inference — annotate function inputs and exported boundaries; let the rest infer
  • type vs interface (either is fine; be consistent)
  • Union types and literal types — prefer 'a' | 'b' over enum
  • Optional and readonly properties, function types
  • unknown vs any — reach for unknown
  • Narrowing: typeof, in, truthiness, equality
  • tsconfig with strict: true from day one

The beginner traps

  • Annotating every variable. const n: number = 5 is noise.
  • Using any to make an error go away — it disables checking for everything downstream
  • Starting without strict, then retrofitting it later
  • Casting with as to silence the compiler when it's right and you're wrong

Checkpoint

Convert a 200-line JavaScript file to TypeScript under strict with zero any and zero as casts. If you can't, the types are usually telling you the code has a real ambiguity.

Stage 02 · Language20–35 h

TypeScript that earns its keep

This is where TypeScript stops being paperwork and starts deleting whole categories of bug. If you learn one thing on this slide, learn discriminated unions.

Worth real effort

  • Generics — functions and types with parameters
  • Discriminated unions — make impossible states impossible
  • keyof, typeof, indexed access T[K]
  • Utility types: Partial, Pick, Omit, Record, ReturnType, Awaited
  • Type guards and value is T predicates
  • satisfies — check a value against a type without widening it

Read it, don't write it yet

Conditional types, mapped types, template literal types, variance annotations. You need to read these to use good libraries. You almost never need to author them in application code, and trying to is a well-known time sink.

The pattern that pays for the whole stage

// Four booleans = 16 states, most nonsense.
// A union = exactly 4 states, all valid.
type Req<T> =
  | { status: 'idle' }
  | { status: 'loading' }
  | { status: 'error'; error: string }
  | { status: 'done';  data: T };

function view(r: Req<User>) {
  if (r.status === 'done')  return r.data.name;
  if (r.status === 'error') return r.error;
  // r.data here → compile error. Good.
  return '…';
}
▲ Checkpoint. Model a request lifecycle as a discriminated union and prove the compiler rejects reading data while loading. Then find the isLoading / data / error triple somewhere in your own code and replace it.
Stage 03 · React30–50 h

The React mental model

React is one idea: UI is a function of state. You change state; React works out the DOM. Nearly every React bug is a violation of that sentence.

The model

  • Components are functions: props in, UI out
  • JSX and its rules (expressions, not statements)
  • useState and immutable updates
  • Lists and stable keys — never the array index
  • Controlled inputs and forms
  • Lifting state up; passing children down
  • Derive, don't duplicate, state
  • useContext for genuinely global things
  • useRef for DOM nodes and non-render values
  • Changing key to reset a subtree

Effects, last and least

Learn useEffect after everything else, and learn it mainly to know when not to use it. An effect is for synchronising with something outside React — a subscription, a timer, a non-React widget.

  • Transforming data for render
  • Resetting state when a prop changes
  • Chaining effects that set state
  • Fetching data, in most modern setups

The page You Might Not Need an Effect in the React docs has the highest value-per-minute of anything in this deck.

Checkpoint

Build a filterable, sortable list with a detail view and a form — using no useEffect at all. Every displayed value should be either state or computed from state during render.

Don't yet

  • Reach for useMemo/useCallback before measuring
  • Add a state library
  • Learn class components — they're legacy
  • Learn Redux "because it's on job ads"
Stage 04 · React10–15 h

React and TypeScript together

A short stage, because if stages 02 and 03 landed there is little new here — just a handful of conventions worth copying rather than deriving.

The conventions

  • Type props as a plain type; skip React.FC
  • React.ReactNode for children
  • Event types: React.ChangeEvent<HTMLInputElement>, MouseEvent
  • ComponentProps<typeof Button> to reuse another component's props
  • Generic components for lists and tables
  • Discriminated union props for variants that can't mix
  • useState<T | null>(null) when the initial value lies

Union props, so bad calls don't compile

type Props =
  | { kind: 'link';   href: string }
  | { kind: 'button'; onClick: () => void };

// <Cta kind="link" onClick={…} /> → error
// <Cta kind="link" />            → error, href missing
◆ Checkpoint. Write a <Table> generic over its row type where the column keys are constrained to keyof Row — so a typo in a column name is a compile error, not an empty cell.
Stage 05 · Framework25–40 h

Next.js: routing and rendering

React draws components. Next.js answers the harder questions: which URL, rendered where, with what data, cached how long.

Routing

  • Files are routes; folders are segments
  • Dynamic [id] and catch-all [...slug]
  • Nested layouts that persist across navigation
  • loading, error, not-found conventions
  • Route handlers for HTTP endpoints
  • Middleware for auth, locale, redirects

Rendering & data

  • Server vs Client Components — the concept to actually understand
  • 'use client' and where the boundary belongs
  • Static, dynamic, and incremental regeneration
  • Caching and revalidation — the most common source of "why is this stale"
  • Server Actions for mutations
  • Streaming with Suspense
  • Metadata, images, fonts

Checkpoint

Ship a small app with a static page, a dynamic detail page, a form that mutates through a Server Action, and a deliberate cache-revalidation strategy you can explain out loud. If you can't explain when a page re-renders and why, keep going.

Best single resource

The official Next.js Dashboard App course — 16 chapters, builds one real app, covers styling through auth and metadata. Free.

Stage 05 · aside

App Router or Pages Router?

This question causes more paralysis than it deserves, and most of the internet answers it wrongly in one direction or the other.

What's actually true

  • App Router is where Next.js is going. New features land there first; it's what new projects should use and what the docs lead with.
  • Pages Router is not deprecated. It's still documented and supported, and the two routers are explicitly designed to co-exist in one app so teams can migrate page by page.
  • You will meet Pages Router in real jobs. A large share of production Next.js was written before the App Router and hasn't moved.

What to do

  • Learn App Router first. Server Components are the harder, more transferable idea, and Pages Router is easier to pick up afterwards than the reverse.
  • Then learn the Pages Router mapping — an afternoon: getServerSideProps → server-side fetch, _app/_document → root layout, pages/api → route handlers.
  • Don't migrate a working app to practise. Migration is a project, not an exercise.
▲ Careful with tutorials. Search results for "Next.js tutorial" mix the two routers freely, often without saying which. If a tutorial uses getServerSideProps or pages/_app.js, it's Pages Router — fine, but know what you're reading.
Stage 06 · Framework15–25 h

Data and state

One distinction fixes most front-end architecture arguments: server state is a cache, UI state is a value. Confusing them is why so many apps have a giant global store full of API responses.

Kind of stateWhat it really isWhere it belongs
Server data A cached copy of something you don't own. Can go stale, needs refetch, loading and error states. Server Components, or a query cache like TanStack Query. Not a global store.
URL state Filters, tabs, pagination, search. Should survive refresh and be shareable. The URL. Search params are state, and free to implement.
Form state In-progress edits plus validation. Local state, or React Hook Form; validate with a schema library like Zod, shared with the server.
Local UI state Is the drawer open, which row is hovered. useState, as close to the component as possible.
Genuinely global UI state Theme, current user, feature flags, cart. Context, or a small store — Zustand, Redux Toolkit, MobX. Usually far less than you expect.
▲ Checkpoint. Build a list where filters and pagination live in the URL, server data comes from a cache with real loading and error states, and your global store is either tiny or absent. Then ask which state you could delete because it's derivable.
Stage 07 · Framework10–15 h

Styling and design systems

Every styling library is a way of writing CSS. If the CSS underneath is shaky, the library just relocates the confusion.

Non-negotiable CSS

  • Box model, flexbox, grid
  • Cascade, specificity, inheritance
  • Custom properties (--vars)
  • Media & container queries
  • Logical properties (inline-start) — this is how RTL works
  • prefers-color-scheme, prefers-reduced-motion

Then pick exactly one

  • Tailwind — the current default. Fast, verbose markup, works everywhere.
  • CSS Modules — plain CSS, scoped. Boring in the good way; zero runtime.
  • CSS-in-JS (Emotion, styled-components) — huge in existing codebases. Note it generally needs a client boundary, so it interacts awkwardly with Server Components.

Learn one deeply. Knowing three shallowly helps nobody.

Then the real skill

  • Design tokens — colour, type, spacing as named values, never literals
  • Consuming a component library instead of rebuilding it
  • Variants without prop explosions
  • Theming, including dark mode as a designed state rather than an inversion
  • RTL layout if you ever ship Arabic or Hebrew
Stage 08 · Craft20–30 h

Testing, accessibility, internationalisation

The stage that separates "it works on my machine" from "it works for everyone, and still will next month".

Testing

  • Vitest or Jest as the runner
  • React Testing Library — query by role and label, the way a user finds things
  • Test behaviour, not implementation. No assertions on internal state.
  • Playwright or Cypress for a few real end-to-end flows
  • Type-check and lint in CI — cheapest tests you own

Accessibility

  • Semantic HTML first — most of a11y is not using <div> for everything
  • Labels on every input; alt text that says something
  • Keyboard operability and visible focus
  • Colour contrast, and never colour as the only signal
  • ARIA only when semantics genuinely run out
  • Try your app with the mouse unplugged

Internationalisation

  • No hardcoded user-facing strings, from the first commit
  • Locale-aware dates, numbers, currency (Intl)
  • Pluralisation rules differ per language
  • RTL is a layout concern, not a text concern
  • Text expands — German is long, and your button isn't
◆ Checkpoint. Take a feature you've built and add tests for its behaviour, operate it entirely by keyboard, and run it in a second language including one RTL locale. Everything you find is a bug you'd otherwise have shipped.
Stage 09 · Craft20–40 h

Production concerns

Measure, then optimise. Every performance rule here becomes wrong for some app, and the profiler is the only thing that knows which.

Performance

  • Core Web Vitals: LCP (load), INP (responsiveness), CLS (stability)
  • Bundle analysis — find the 300 KB date library you didn't know about
  • Code splitting and dynamic imports for heavy, rarely-used UI
  • Image and font optimisation; these are usually the biggest wins
  • React DevTools Profiler before adding a single useMemo
  • The React Compiler handles much of what manual memoisation used to

Operations

  • Error monitoring and source maps — know it broke before users tell you
  • Real-user monitoring, not just lab scores
  • Environment config and secret handling
  • Feature flags, so deploy and release come apart
  • CI: typecheck, lint, test, build, preview deploy
  • Caching layers: browser, CDN, framework, data. Know which one lied to you.
▲ Checkpoint. Take one of your own apps, measure it, find the single worst metric, fix that one thing, and measure again. One honest before-and-after teaches more than any performance checklist.

Failure modes

Seven ways this goes wrong

Each of these is common, and each costs weeks to months. They're worth reading before you start, not after.

  • Tutorial hell. Finishing courses feels like progress. Nothing you haven't built from an empty folder is yours yet.
  • Skipping the fundamentals. Weak JavaScript and weak CSS surface later as "React is confusing" and "styling is impossible".
  • Learning tools before problems. Redux, monorepos, micro-frontends solve pains you haven't felt. Feel the pain first; the tool then makes sense in an hour.
  • The useEffect reflex. The most common structural mistake in React code. Reach for it last, not first.
  • any as an escape hatch. Each one silently disables checking for everything downstream. If a type is hard, the design is usually unclear.
  • Framework hopping. Switching stacks when it gets hard resets you to zero. Difficulty is the curve, not the wrong choice.
  • Building only greenfield toys. Real work is reading unfamiliar code and changing it safely. That's a separate skill, and no tutorial trains it.
▲ If you recognise several of these, the fix is nearly always the same: pick one project slightly beyond you and finish it, badly, without starting anything else.
Practice

Five projects, in this order

Each one is chosen to force a specific lesson. Finish each before starting the next — an unfinished project teaches almost nothing.

A personal site with no data

Routing, layouts, styling, deployment, and the whole "get it on the internet" pipeline — with zero state to hide behind. Teaches: the framework's shape.

A TypeScript utility library, no UI at all

A date helper, a small parser, a validation function. Generics, unions, unit tests, published types. Teaches: TypeScript, uncontaminated by React.

A CRUD app against a real API

Forms, validation, loading and error states, optimistic updates. Use somebody else's messy public API, not a perfect mock. Teaches: async reality.

A dashboard with auth and server data

Login, protected routes, server-side data, filters and pagination in the URL, charts, a11y, dark mode. Teaches: this is roughly what the job is.

A change to a codebase you didn't write

An open-source issue, or a feature in your team's app. Read, locate, change without breaking, match existing conventions. Teaches: the actual daily skill — and it's the one nobody practises.

◆ Ship each one. A deployed URL forces the last unglamorous 20% — build config, env vars, error states, the empty case, the slow network — which is where a surprising amount of the learning actually is.

What this looks like in a production codebase

A real app never matches a tutorial. Here's the shape of one working Next.js product, and which stages of this path each part actually draws on.

MonorepoBusiness logic in one package, UI in another. Stage 00–02.

How to actually onboard onto one

  • Find the entry points first. Read the route files. They tell you what the product does far faster than the component tree.
  • Trace one feature end to end — URL → page → container → components → store → API. One vertical slice beats a broad skim.
  • Read the conventions docs before writing anything. Matching the existing pattern matters more than your preferred pattern.
  • Make your first change tiny and get it reviewed. You're testing your understanding, not the change.
  • Ask what's load-bearing. Every codebase has a piece nobody touches. Find out which before you touch it.
◆ The fastest onboarding tool is a map of the repo: what each top-level folder is for, which routes exist, and what renders them — before you read a single component.

Self-assessment

What "done" actually looks like

Not a certificate. These are the questions a senior engineer's answers reveal in about ten minutes of conversation.

You've genuinely got the language

  • You can explain why mutating state doesn't re-render
  • You model states as a union rather than three booleans
  • You reach for unknown, and any feels like a smell
  • You let inference work and only annotate boundaries

You've genuinely got React

  • You can say what causes a re-render, and check rather than guess
  • Your first instinct for new state is "can I derive this?"
  • You write effects rarely, and can justify each one
  • You reach for composition before configuration props

You've genuinely got the framework

  • You can explain where a given component renders, and why
  • You can debug a stale value through every caching layer
  • You put filters in the URL without being asked
  • You know which state belongs on the server

You're ready for production work

  • You can operate your own UI with the keyboard only
  • You measure before optimising, every time
  • You can land a small change in an unfamiliar codebase and match its conventions
  • You can say "I don't know yet" and then go find out
◆ The honest closing note. Nobody holds all of this at once, and the people who look like they do are just further along one particular path. Pick the next stage, build the thing, ship it badly, repeat. That's the whole method.
